# Choose the Right Answer I

[← All classes and lessons](index.md)

**Class context:** 120 MINUTES \| LIECHTENAUER &amp; MEYER LENS

## The problem, behavior, and boundaries

**A \| CLASS SNAPSHOT**

The class develops choice among four broad defensive families. It is an assessment and decision lesson, not a test of named techniques.

**CLASS TITLE:** Choose the Right Answer I
{: #class-title}

**CENTRAL QUESTION:** When an opponent attacks, how do I choose among counterattacking, parry-riposte, evading, or withdrawing?
{: #central-question}

**END-OF-CLASS BEHAVIOR:** Students recognize attack commitment, distance, line, and available time, then select a response that removes the threat and produces a clean outcome rather than forcing a preferred answer.
{: #end-of-class-behavior}

**HISTORICAL ANCHOR:** The Liechtenauer tradition's Before, After, and Indes, paired with Meyer's warning that even a good technique fails when used without the right opportunity.
{: #historical-anchor}

## Target-action success test

**A \| SUCCESS EVIDENCE**

The target behavior counts only when all four observable conditions are present.

| REQUIREMENT | OBSERVABLE STANDARD |
| --- | --- |
| Recognition | The student identifies the attack early enough to perceive distance, line, and commitment. |
| Selection | The response fits the available time and geometry: intercept, parry-answer, evade, or withdraw. |
| Execution | The chosen response genuinely removes or avoids the incoming threat. |
| Adaptation | The student changes or abandons the answer when the attack changes and avoids the double. |

**NO DOUBLE:** A result that leaves both fencers validly hit does not satisfy the lesson, even if one blade arrived first.

### Primary functional model

Use the simplest response that solves the present attack. More ambitious actions require earlier information and better geometry; when those are absent, choose a larger safety margin.

### Instructor hypothesis

Students will force their favorite response, label the choice only after the fact, or treat retreat as failure even when it is the safest solution.

## Arrival and orientation

**C \| BLOCK 1 \| 0:00-0:05**

Students understand the problem, success test, and class arc.

**OPENING SENTENCE:** "Tonight we are solving one problem: When an opponent attacks, how do I choose among counterattacking, parry-riposte, evading, or withdrawing?"
{: #opening-sentence}

**SUCCESS DEFINITION:** State the four-part success test in plain language and make clear that a double is a failed solution.
{: #success-definition}

**CLASS ARC:** Experience the problem first; compare what worked; examine one historical lens; increase choice and pressure; test transfer in open fencing.
{: #class-arc}

**DO NOT:** Teach the complete historical solution before the first game. Students should possess the problem before receiving the model.

## Athletic development

**C \| BLOCK 2 \| 0:05-0:20**

React in four directions and arrive balanced enough to continue, rather than making one maximal movement.

**WHAT THIS BLOCK IS:** A short movement lesson, not conditioning. Demonstrate each exercise once, keep the volume low, and stop or regress when posture or landing quality deteriorates.
{: #what-this-block-is}

**TARGET QUALITY:** React in four directions and arrive balanced enough to continue, rather than making one maximal movement.
{: #target-quality}

### Exercise sequence

| EXERCISE | DOSE | COACHING FOCUS | MOVEMENT PURPOSE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Four-direction stance step | 2 x 4 each direction | Short travel; feet do not tangle; eyes forward | Prepare multiple defensive movement options |
| Drop-step and recover | 2 x 4 each lead | Move center first; recover under control | Rehearse creating emergency distance without falling backward |
| Lateral bound or step-and- stick | 2 x 3 each side | Quiet landing; stable knee; usable posture | Prepare evasive displacement |
| Four-choice partner reaction | 3 x 20 seconds each | Name cue after moving; no guessing | Connect visual information to different movement families |

### How to perform the exercises

### 1. FOUR-DIRECTION STANCE STEP

From fencing stance, take one short step forward, back, left-diagonal, or right-diagonal, returning to center after each. Keep the feet from crossing and finish every step ready to move again. Cue: 'One step, one stable shape.'

### 2. DROP-STEP AND RECOVER

From a comfortable stance, let the rear foot move backward first while the hips travel with it, then bring the front foot back enough to restore stance. Do not lean the shoulders behind the hips. Pause, then advance back to start. Cue: 'Move the center, then rebuild the stance.'

### 3. LATERAL BOUND OR STEP-AND-STICK

Push gently from one leg and travel a small distance sideways to land on the opposite leg, holding for two seconds. Students who should not jump use a quick lateral step and the same hold. Knee tracks over toes; chest stays controlled. Cue: 'Move enough to clear the line, then own the landing.'

### 4. FOUR-CHOICE PARTNER REACTION

Partners face each other without swords. Leader shows one of four clear cues: straight hand forward, diagonal hand left, diagonal hand right, or both hands advancing. Mover responds with a short intercepting step, parry-shaped hand action, lateral evade, or withdrawal step respectively. After moving, the student names the cue they saw. Keep speed moderate. Cue: 'See, choose, arrive balanced.'

**REGRESSION AND STOP RULE:** Reduce speed, distance, or impact; replace jumps with controlled steps. Stop for pain, dizziness, repeated loss of balance, loud uncontrolled landings, or a student who cannot maintain the described posture.
{: #regression-and-stop-rule}

## Representative problem exposure: Four Answers

**C \| BLOCK 3 \| 0:20-0:40**

Make response selection visible without requiring open-sparring complexity.

**STARTING STATE:** Attacker begins at variable but controlled distance and may deliver one committed high cut.
{: #starting-state}

**PLAYER GOALS:** Defender chooses counterattack, parry-riposte, evade-and-hit, or withdrawal. Attacker recovers honestly.
{: #player-goals}

**RULES AND CONSTRAINTS:** Attacker varies side, distance, and rhythm but no feints. Defender is never required to score if safe withdrawal is appropriate.
{: #rules-and-constraints}

**SCORING:** Clean threat removal plus appropriate score 2; safe no-hit resolution 1; attacker clean hit 1; double 0.
{: #scoring}

**DOSE AND ROTATION:** 4 minutes each role; change partners and repeat.
{: #dose-and-rotation}

**INSTRUCTOR OBSERVES:** Favorite-answer bias, response too ambitious for timing, retreat without posture, and after-the-fact rationalization.
{: #instructor-observes}

**COACH POSTURE:** Observe across the room before intervening. Record common patterns and let several exchanges occur before deciding what the class needs.

## Guided coaching

**C \| BLOCK 4 \| 0:40-0:55**

Use the first game's evidence to provide the smallest intervention that improves the class.

### Debrief questions

1. What information made one answer safer than another?

2. Which answer required the earliest decision?

3. When did withdrawal preserve a better future opportunity?

4. What caused a technically correct action to fail?

**PRIMARY INTERVENTION:** Build a simple decision hierarchy: can you intercept with cover? If not, can you parry and answer? If the line is avoidable, can you evade while staying ready? If information is late or geometry poor, create distance. Cue: 'Solve the threat you actually have.'
{: #primary-intervention}

**VARIABLE REHEARSAL:** Partners re-test the target problem at approximately 60-70% intensity. Keep at least one meaningful variable - side, rhythm, distance, pressure, or response - rather than feeding identical repetitions.
{: #variable-rehearsal}

**FEEDBACK PLAN:** Use one cue at a time. Correct immediate danger at once; otherwise give feedback between sets of three attempts. Ask the student to identify the information that shaped the action.
{: #feedback-plan}

**AVOID:** Turning the block into a long lecture or adding several fixes before students can test the first one.

## Historical integration: Liechtenauer &amp; Meyer Lens

**C \| BLOCK 5 \| 0:55-1:05**

Connect the experienced tactical problem to a documented historical principle without claiming that the source prescribes the modern game.

**SOURCE IDENTIFICATION:** The Liechtenauer tradition's Before, After, and Indes, paired with Meyer's warning that even a good technique fails when used without the right opportunity.
{: #source-identification}

**VERIFIED HISTORICAL CLAIM:** The Liechtenauer gloss tradition treats Before and After as foundational and uses Indes to regain initiative while responding. Meyer likewise emphasizes that no sequence, however good, is useful without the proper opportunity. [1][2]
{: #verified-historical-claim}

**MODERN TRAINING INTERPRETATION:** Show the same high cut at four distances and timings. Early, clear commitment may permit a covered counterattack; moderate time supports parry-riposte; a line that can be cleared supports evasion; late recognition or poor geometry favors withdrawal. The four-family decision tool is modern, while the historical lens concerns initiative, timely response, and opportunity.
{: #modern-training-interpretation}

**DEMONSTRATION:** Show three slow examples: one successful application, one common failure, and one case in which the action should be declined. Let students rehearse briefly, then return to live choice.
{: #demonstration}

**EVIDENCE LINE:** Historical text supplies the cited principle or action. The success test, games, scoring, cues, and safety adaptations are modern coaching design.

## Progressive constraints

**C \| BLOCK 6 \| 1:05-1:30**

Increase uncertainty while preserving the lesson's central information-action relationship.

### Game 2 - Distance Changes the Answer \| 12 minutes

Show that the same attack may require a different solution at a different distance.

 Attacker secretly chooses close edge, normal edge, or stretched entry before a committed high cut.  Defender selects a response suited to actual distance and remains safe.  Either side high cut; no feints; exchange may continue through one immediate answer.  Appropriate clean solution 2; safe disengagement 1; attacker clean hit 1; double 0.  5 minutes each role; 2 minutes transition.

### Game 3 - Decision Under Uncertainty \| 13 minutes

Test whether choices survive a more representative attack environment.

 Free onset; designated attacker initiates within eight seconds and may use one feint.  Defender solves the attack with any safe family and continues only while the opportunity remains.  Normal movement; continue to clean hit, double, or safe separation; no bonus for a named technique.  Clean defensive solution 2; safe withdrawal/reset 1; attacker clean hit 1; double 0.  Change attacker every 3 minutes; final minute reset.

**PROGRESSION TRIGGER:** Remove designated attacker and scoring categories.
{: #progression-trigger}

**REGRESSION TRIGGER:** Remove feint and restrict attack to one side.
{: #regression-trigger}

## Open sparring

**C \| BLOCK 7 \| 1:30-1:55**

Test whether the behavior appears without forced actions or bonus scoring.

**FORMAT:** Five 4-minute rounds with approximately 1 minute between rounds for rotation and brief feedback. Use normal open-fencing rules and the club's usual intensity agreement.
{: #format}

**SHARED OBJECTIVE:** "Fence normally. Do not hunt a particular defense. Notice the attack's line, distance, and commitment, then use the least complicated answer that genuinely keeps you safe."
{: #shared-objective}

**PAIRING PLAN:** Use compatible control and size, then rotate across different rhythms and preferences. Supervise novice pairings and avoid a large mismatch solely to complete the rotation.
{: #pairing-plan}

### Coach records

 Sees line, distance, and commitment  Choice fits time and geometry  Threat is removed or avoided  Uses simplest adequate response  Changes answer as attack changes  Clean resolution; doubles decline  Chooses freely without bonuses

**FEEDBACK:** Observe during rounds and coach mainly between them. Open sparring is not another constrained game.

## Reflection and close

**C \| BLOCK 8 \| 1:55-2:00**

Students consolidate one usable cue and one testable next action.

### Whole-group questions

1. Which cue most influenced your choice?

2. Which answer did you overuse?

3. When did a safe withdrawal create a better next exchange?

**INDIVIDUAL NEXT ACTION:** Each student states one small, observable action they will test the next time this problem appears.
{: #individual-next-action}

**INSTRUCTOR CLOSE:** Restate the functional success test, not a technique name. Record any safety issue, equipment problem, or student who needs a lower-intensity follow-up.
{: #instructor-close}

**END ON TIME:** Do not let the final sparring round consume reflection. The class is incomplete until students name the information that shaped their decision.

## Plan modifications before the room forces them

**E \| SAFETY AND ADAPTATION**

Preserve the information and decision while reducing uncertainty, speed, distance, or impact.

| CONDITION | PLANNED RESPONSE |
| --- | --- |
| First-time student | Use a known attack or response, longer preparation time, controlled partner, and score safe resolution as success. |
| Mixed experience | Give beginners less uncertainty; give experienced students more choice or rhythm variation, not greater impact. |
| Odd attendance | Use attacker, defender, and observer. Observer applies the four-part success test, then roles rotate. |
| Limited space | Use narrow lanes; prohibit passing and large leaps; emphasize compact footwork and controlled stops. |
| Injury / low impact | Replace bounds with steps; reduce speed and contact; permit observer or limited-role participation. |
| Large attendance | Create lanes, assign a senior student to each cluster, and synchronize changes with one timer. |
| Game breaks down | Remove one variable, increase distance, slow the action, or return to a known starting state. |
| Time loss | Compress explanation and one role interval; preserve open transfer and reflection. |

## What to watch without interrupting every exchange

**F \| INSTRUCTOR OBSERVATION**

Use the checklist to distinguish learning from mere scoring.

| DOMAIN | LOOK FOR | COMMON FAILURE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognition | Sees line, distance, and commitment | Reacts only when blade is close |
| Selection | Choice fits time and geometry | Forces favorite defense |
| Defense | Threat is removed or avoided | Relies on scoring first |
| Complexity | Uses simplest adequate response | Adds unnecessary actions |
| Adaptation | Changes answer as attack changes | Finishes preplanned script |
| Outcome | Clean resolution; doubles decline | Counts doubles as partial success |
| Transfer | Chooses freely without bonuses | Needs instructor to name response |

### Between-round feedback formula

Observation -&gt; question -&gt; one cue -&gt; re-test. Name what happened, ask what information the student perceived, offer one change, and let the next round test it.

### Class-level success evidence

 Cleaner solutions and fewer doubles from Game 1 to Game 3.  Students can name at least one reliable cue.  The target behavior appears selectively in open fencing.  Students can explain why an unsafe score does not satisfy the lesson.  Beginners show safer structure even when they do not score.
