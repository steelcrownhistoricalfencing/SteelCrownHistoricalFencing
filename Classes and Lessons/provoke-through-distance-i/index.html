    Provoke Through Distance I

    Footwork feints and prepared traps

    LESSON | INSTRUCTORS AUDIENCE

    Chris Mineweaser | November 2026 | Version 2.0

    ← All classes and lessons

    Class context: 120 MINUTES | LONGSWORD | MEYER HISTORICAL LENS

    Central question

    How can I sell an entry, a retreat, or an attack beat so the opponent commits to the wrong answer, then take the opening cleanly?

    Footwork feints and prepared traps

    LESSON OVERVIEW

    Use movement to suggest an intention you do not fully carry out. Prepare an answer to the mistake you expect, then confirm it before finishing.

    Feint Mistake you are inviting Prepared answer
    False step-in They attack the entry they think is arriving, but their attack falls short. Make the attack miss with a small withdrawal; answer its recovery if reachable.
    Half-step back They believe you are giving ground and press forward into your prepared distance. Catch an exposed advance with a covered action; defend first if they press with an attack.
    Stomp or footfall feint They defend on the footfall, before the real attack begins. Deliver the real attack on the following beat to the opening their early defense exposes.

    Student outcomes: Students make a convincing footwork cue without losing balance or defensive options; identify the specific attack, chase, or early parry it draws; execute a prepared follow-up at usable distance; and recover or change plan when the trigger never appears. A double or an attack into a closed line does not meet the outcome.

    Plan the trap before you start: Know what you are trying to make them believe and what reply you are ready to exploit. A rehearsed answer is useful. Treating the expected reply as guaranteed is the error. This is deliberate provocation, not just moving around to see what happens.

    Make the signal credible: Mix genuine entries and attacks with feints. If every step is false, an opponent can simply ignore it. Use familiar attacks and defenses so the learning stays on deception, distance, and timing.

    Shared practice format: Use club-required longsword equipment, permitted targets and actions, and agreed intensity. Work short phrases without points. Reset on a clear touch, double, separation, loss of control, or halt under normal club conventions. Never add a late hit after halt.

    Prepare to sell movement without falling into it

    OPENING 0:00-0:05 | WARM-UP 0:05-0:15

    The physical job is to start, brake, and change the timing of an action while remaining able to defend.

    Say to students: "Tonight we are lying with the feet. A step can sell an entry that never quite arrives. A half-step back can invite someone to chase. A stomp can make them defend before the cut. Decide what mistake you want, be ready for it, and check that they actually make it."

    Before partner work: Confirm equipment, contact level, permitted targets, stop call, and reset conditions. Leave clear stopping space. Match partners for compatible control and pace. Make it easy to request a slower version or a break.

    Time Preparation What to preserve
    0:05-0:10 Familiar general movement, easy forward/back steps, then brief low hops or controlled step-and-stop actions once warm. Balance and the ability to brake; short efforts with recovery.
    0:10-0:15 Without swords, practise a shallow false start, a short retreat that can stop, and a small accented footfall. Partners watch which movement looks like a real entry. A believable cue with recoverable feet. An accented step is not a hard heel slam.

    Coach watches: Can the fencer suggest forward commitment without pitching the head and torso into danger? Can a half-step retreat stop without needing another backward step? Can the footfall be distinct without a jump or a painful impact?

    Lower demand and gear transition: Replace hops with steps and replace the stomp with a light step or body accent. Use smaller movement and more rest where needed. Begin gear transition during the end of this block; complete all checks before sword exchanges. Do not sacrifice equipment checks to the clock.

    Make the real entry and the false entry look related

    CALIBRATION 0:15-0:25

    The opponent should have a reason to believe the footwork cue. Start with the same familiar setup for both possibilities.

    Setup: Full club-required equipment, controlled pace, and a clear lane. Partners begin near the edge of a familiar attacking action. Confirm that the real action can reach with an appropriate step, while the false version can still stop or recover.

    Initiator: Sometimes use the normal entry and a controlled cut. Sometimes show the beginning of that same entry but withhold the cut and remain recoverable. Avoid a huge blade feint: the first deceptive cue should come from the feet, body, or rhythm.

    Responder: Try to prevent a clean hit and take your own appropriate opportunity. You may defend, attack, yield, or hold. Read the whole threat rather than guessing that every step is real or that every step is false.

    How to run it: Demonstrate a few slow examples, then use 90-second roles and switch immediately. Change partners after a complete pair of roles where practical. No scorekeeping. Short phrases and the shared halt/reset conventions apply.

    What the coach is looking for: Can the fencer make the opening cue similar while keeping the false action shorter or less committed? Does the partner react to the offered entry? Does the fencer remain able to defend if the partner attacks the feint?

    Bridge to the traps: Ask: 'What did the step make you expect?' Then narrow the next block to one anticipated response: an attack aimed at the entry. Do not require the responder to be fooled every attempt.

    Sell the entry and draw the attack short

    TRAP 1 0:25-0:45

    Make the opponent attack the place they expect you to occupy, then answer while they recover.

    Starting problem: Begin where a normal entry would bring you into reach. Use a credible approach cue: a small forward step, a step-start, or a convincing change of rhythm. Retain enough balance and weapon readiness to withdraw or defend.

    Worked example: Show the beginning of an entry. They commit an attack to meet it. Do not complete the entry: take just enough distance away for the attack to miss, then return a familiar controlled cut as their attack finishes or recovers. If their blade still threatens you, defend before answering.

    The important distance: Too far away, the feint has no threat. Too large a retreat, and your answer cannot reach. Find a small distance change that makes their reach judgment wrong while leaving a usable answer. If no safe answer is reachable, recover rather than lunge after it.

    First five minutes: Use a known, controlled attack response to calibrate the distance and withdrawal. Switch roles after a few attempts. The responder attacks normally within agreed control; do not ask them to lunge farther, lock a joint, or lose balance for the drill.

    Remaining fifteen minutes: The initiator mixes genuine entry attacks with false entries. The responder may attack, defend, yield, or ignore the cue and tries to deny a clean finish. Use 90-second roles and partner changes after both roles. The trap must work against a choice, not only a feed.

    Coach cue and success test: "Sell the entry; stay able to leave; catch the recovery." Look for the attack being drawn early or short, the fencer withdrawing in control, and a reachable protected answer. A miss by the opponent is useful evidence, but it does not automatically make your next hit available.

    Give half a step and invite the chase

    TRAP 2 0:45-1:05

    Suggest that you are giving up ground while staying ready to stop and act as they press the apparent advantage.

    Set the invitation: Begin at a distance where the opponent wants to follow if you retreat. Give a small, believable half-step back rather than a large escape. Keep a familiar guard and a stance that can stop; you are selling withdrawal, not surrendering balance or deliberately exposing your head.

    Worked example: You give half a step. They step after you to maintain pressure, with the leading arm exposed and no live attack closing the line. Stop yielding and make a compact covered cut to that available forearm, using the club's permitted targets and control standard. Recover with the weapon still able to protect you.

    If they press with an attack: The trap is not permission to hit through the incoming blade. Use a familiar parry and answer, or take the attack out of reach if possible. If they follow with a closed line, hold safely, or do not follow at all, the expected opening has not appeared.

    First five minutes: Have the partner follow a short retreat with a normal, balanced advance while retaining defensive ability. Calibrate when the exposed target becomes reachable. Also show a following attack so the initiator can distinguish an advance from a live threat.

    Remaining fifteen minutes: The responder chooses whether to hold, follow, or attack and may protect against the prepared answer. The initiator may genuinely withdraw instead of always springing the trap. Use 90-second roles; switch partners after both roles. Keep the same short-phrase stop rules.

    Coach cue and success test: "Give a little ground, not the decision." Look for a believable invitation, a real forward commitment, and an answer fitted to the weapon situation. If the initiator must reverse wildly or trade hits, simplify the distance or restore the familiar defense.

    Prepare the answer but verify the trigger

    COACHING AND MEYER LENS 1:05-1:15

    Use one observed exchange to show the difference between a planned trap and blindly completing a sequence.

    Coaching reset: Ask: 'What were you trying to make them believe? What reply were you ready for? Did that reply actually happen?' Demonstrate one response that fits the trap and one that does not. Change the finish when the trigger changes.

    Historical connection in plain English: In Sword, Chapter 7, Meyer describes suggesting a broad approach while keeping the feet close, and disguising the speed of an approach. In Chapter 6's withdrawal discussion, he describes appearing to leave, drawing pursuit, letting the pursuing cut miss, and answering. Paraphrase, not quotation. [1]

    Application boundary: These passages support deceptive approach and withdrawal. Today's half-step and stomp drills are modern training choices, not named Meyer sequences. The footfall feint is a preparatory cue; it is not a rule to detach every real cut from its supporting movement.

    Demonstrate the next timing problem: Contrast a normal step-and-cut with an accented step followed by a withheld cut. Let the partner show an early defense on the footfall, then show the real attack on the following beat. Repeat with the partner holding a sound guard: no opening means no automatic finish.

    Source: [1] Meyer, Joachim (1570), Sword, Chapters 6-7, folios I.23r.4-I.24r.2. Rebecca L. R. Garber translation (2023), Wiktenauer. Read the source text .

    Sell the attack beat with a stomp

    TRAP 3 1:15-1:35

    Use the footfall and body cue to draw an early defense, then deliver the real attack on the following beat.

    Setup: Use a familiar guard and a calibrated distance where a genuine step-and-cut would be believable. Start with controlled, readable actions. The initiator can actually enter and attack; the stomp is not a noise made far outside any credible threat.

    First beat: Make a small, emphatic forward step or stomp with the body cue of an entry. Keep the weapon available but withhold the expected cut. The intended mistake is a defensive movement made on the footfall instead of on the real attack.

    Following beat: As an early parry sweeps away from the line or opens another target, deliver the prepared controlled cut to that actual opening. The delay is a short timing offset, not a long pause. If the parry remains sound or the opponent counterattacks, change plan or defend; do not strike into a closed guard.

    First five minutes: Contrast a normal entry with the footfall-first version. The partner initially gives a controlled early parry to make the timing visible, then varies its timing. Let students feel the difference before adding free choices. Switch roles after a few attempts.

    Remaining fifteen minutes: Mix genuine attacks on the normal beat with footfall feints and delayed attacks. The responder may defend early, wait, yield, or attack and tries to deny a clean hit. Use 90-second roles; change partners after both roles. No points and no compulsory reaction.

    Coach cue and boundaries: "Let the foot sell the cut; let their defense choose the finish." Use an accented footfall, not painful heel-pounding, jumping, or uncontrolled forward momentum. A light step or body accent is a valid lower-impact version. Check the blade and opening, not just whether the opponent flinched.

    Use the trap when the opponent gives you the reason

    OPEN FENCING 1:35-1:55 | FINISH 1:55-2:00

    Remove the drill roles. The feint must remain credible against someone who is making their own plan.

    Format: Four four-minute rounds, with partner rotation and water breaks inside the twenty-minute block. Use normal Steel Crown equipment, intensity, permitted actions, and halt conventions. No bonus points for feinting and no requirement to use all three traps.

    Shared objective: "Choose a trap that suits what this opponent expects: entry, retreat, or attack timing. Be ready for the reply you want. If they do something else, do not force the finish."

    Watch for Evidence in normal fencing
    Credibility Genuine entries and attacks remain possible; the feint resembles something the opponent needs to respect.
    Triggered mistake A premature attack, committed chase, or early defensive action follows the cue.
    Prepared answer The fencer takes the specific recovery, exposed advance, or opening created by the early parry.
    Judgment and protection The fencer abandons the trap when necessary, deals with the blade, and avoids a double or uncontrolled finish.

    Feedback between rounds: Name the cue, the response, and what made the answer available or unavailable. Give one useful adjustment rather than asking students to narrate while fencing. Compare relevant opportunities, not simply the number of hits.

    Final five minutes: Allow a calm gear transition and individual feedback. If a group question helps, ask: 'What did you make them expect, and which mistake did you actually catch?' Record what should be revisited next class. One good round is not proof of lasting learning.

    Correct the mechanism that is not working

    COACH REFERENCE

    Keep one trap and one intended reply clear before adding another possibility.

    If you see Useful adjustment
    Nobody believes the feint Check distance and whether real attacks are mixed in. Make the opening cue resemble a genuine action; do not require the partner to react.
    The false entry gets hit Reduce commitment, clarify the starting distance, and retain a familiar defense. Do not solve it by asking the partner to overextend.
    The retreat becomes an escape Reduce it to a half-step and check the reachable target. If the fencer cannot stop comfortably, slow down and simplify.
    Every following step gets attacked Alternate a plain advance, a covered advance, and an incoming attack. Teach the distinction before restoring freer play.
    The stomp produces no opening Check whether the defender actually moved early and uncovered a line. A flinch or the sound alone does not earn the attack.
    The delayed cut meets a counterattack Defend the live threat or recover. Shorten the pause; keep the blade ready instead of waiting helplessly for a second beat.
    The room needs less Use known starts, fewer choices, smaller steps, and a controlled partner. Restore choice after useful attempts are possible.

    Safety and access: Use current club requirements. Stop for loss of control, collisions, equipment issues, or ignored halt calls. No forced overextension, hard heel impacts, or late hits. For odd numbers, rotate an observer who tracks one trigger. Match challenge through timing and choice, not greater impact.

    After class: Which cue was believable? Which expected reply appeared? Did the prepared answer work without a trade? What needs a clearer start or more practice?
