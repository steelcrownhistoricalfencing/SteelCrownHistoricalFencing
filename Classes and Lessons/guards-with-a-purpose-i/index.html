    Guards with a Purpose I

    Use Meyer's guards to prepare a threat, shape an opponent's choices, and remain ready for the next action.

    LESSON | INSTRUCTORS AUDIENCE

    Steel Crown | November 2026 | Version 1.1

    ← All classes and lessons · Illustrated guard reference

    What problem are we solving?

    A student can reproduce a guard and still have no useful action from it. Another student may pass through the same position successfully without knowing its name. Tonight connects the name, the position, and the decision: what does this guard let me do, what does it offer my opponent, and when should I leave it?

    Main point

    A guard is a position of the whole fencer with the sword. Its value depends on the opponent and the distance—not on how closely it resembles a photograph.

    Say to the room: “We are not collecting poses tonight. Pick a position for a reason. Know what you can threaten, what you are leaving available, and what you will do if they take it. If the situation changes, the guard can change too.”

    Meyer's historical descriptions and movement examples supply the vocabulary. Our competition tests, games, and scoring are modern Steel Crown coaching choices, not exercises prescribed in his book. [S1]

    What students should leave able to do

    By the end, a student should be able to:

    • Recognize and form Ochs, Pflug, Tag, and Alber; distinguish Longpoint from Hanging Point.
    • Explain one available threat and one exposure from a chosen guard at the current measure.
    • Use that position to attack, defend, invite a response, or leave, without a large unnecessary reset.
    • Change their answer when the expected response does not appear.
    • Finish an exchange balanced and protected, rather than treating the first touch as sufficient.

    Success is not using every guard or changing guards constantly. A student who uses one or two positions deliberately and survives the opponent's answer has met the practical aim better than someone who cycles through all twelve without purpose.

    The two-hour plan

    Time Block Purpose
    0:00–0:05 Introduce the guard problem Connect position, threat, exposure, and next action.
    0:05–0:15 Movement preparation Prepare balanced entries, stops, and changes.
    0:15–0:35 Guided guard laboratory Learn the four principal guards and two useful connections.
    0:35–0:55 Game 1: Make it earn its place Test what a chosen guard actually makes available.
    0:55–1:03 Debrief and one intervention Identify the decision or preparation that failed.
    1:03–1:10 Change without a reset Connect guard positions through familiar actions.
    1:10–1:30 Game 2: Offer, read, leave Use a guard or transition to influence a live opponent.
    1:30–1:55 Open fencing Test transfer without requiring named guards.
    1:55–2:00 Reflection Link a position to an observed result.

    Prepare movement, not poses

    0:05–0:15. In solo lanes, move between low, extended, and high sword positions. Add a short entry, retreat, or change of direction and stop in a position from which another action is immediately available. Compare holding the hands beside the head with extending them in front: what changes about the next cut or cover?

    Finish with a partner mirroring changes of measure. Each fencer chooses a starting guard and adjusts it only when the distance or available line gives them a reason. Watch for a separate reload before moving or cutting.

    Guided guard laboratory

    0:15–0:35. Use Meyer’s original guard plates. Demonstrate briefly, let students try, and ask a question before giving another explanation.

    First eight minutes: four principal guards. Show Ochs, Pflug, Tag, and Alber using the landmarks in the reference. Have students identify where the hilt and point are, what action is already prepared, and what opening appears available. Show both sides where comfortable. Do not demand that every student make an identical silhouette.

    Position First question Practical caution
    Ochs / Ox Can I threaten or close an upper line without first dropping and reloading? High hands alone do not cover every route to the head or hands.
    Pflug / Plow Can my low point-forward position affect their approach? Do not let the point float harmlessly outside the relationship.
    Tag / Day or High Guard Can I make a credible descending cut from here? A prepared cut is not a shield; distance and timing still matter.
    Alber / Fool Can I recognize the attack this low position makes tempting? An apparent invitation is only useful if I can answer or get out.

    Next six minutes: same guard, different distance. Partners take turns presenting a chosen guard. Outside contact range, the other person walks slowly toward a marked limit and points out which target or blade route looks available. Pause before either person can strike. Move the limit farther out or closer and compare the answers. No attacks in this observation task. Ask: “Did the shape change, or did its usefulness change?”

    Final six minutes: connect to familiar actions. Show Longpoint as the extended point-forward position and Hanging Point as the high, forward-hand position with the blade descending. Demonstrate one familiar controlled cut into a useful extension, then a familiar hanging cover and exit. Name what appears without claiming every extension is a successful cover or every hanging shape is a successful parry.

    Learning check: Ask students to show a named guard, identify one option, and point out one exposure. If they cannot, simplify to two guards before introducing live choice. The full secondary-guard catalogue belongs in the reference, not tonight's memory test.

    When two guards look alike: Use the reference's look-alike comparisons. Ask students to identify which side of the body the hands occupy, whether they are beside the head or extended in front, and where the point goes. Use the foreground fencers identified in each plate caption; distinguish the reader’s left/right from the fencer’s own sides. Compare one pair at a time rather than adding every secondary guard to tonight's memory load.

    Game 1: Make it earn its place

    0:35–0:55. The purpose is to discover what a guard can accomplish against a partner who is trying to solve it, rather than feeding its preferred response.

    Setup and roles: Use cuts only for this first comparison. A starts just outside a comfortable single-entry cutting distance in one of the four principal guards. B starts in any familiar useful position. A names the guard and tells the observer one threat and one exposure; B does not have to attack that exposure.

    The exchange: B initiates with one controlled cutting attack to either of two agreed targets, changes distance, or declines the entry. A may defend and answer, make a covered cut into a genuine opening, or leave. Neither must preserve the original guard. Allow one immediate answer to test the finish, then reset. No extended combinations or pursuit.

    How to run it: Use two minutes for demonstration and calibration. Run four four-minute sets, with thirty seconds of feedback between sets and a final short debrief. Within each set, switch A and B after two minutes. In early sets choose between Tag and Alber; then compare Ochs and Pflug. If a student already has a useful familiar guard, allow it as a comparison rather than forcing an unfamiliar position under pressure.

    Scoring: A earns one point for a clean touch that survives the permitted answer. B earns one for their own clean touch under the same condition. Doubles and accepted immediate returns score zero for both. A safe exit is a successful defensive decision but does not score a hit. If B declines, A may maintain safe measure and reset; do not chase simply to force a result.

    Observer watches: Did the chosen position remove an extra preparation, influence B's entry, or support a safe answer? Was the failure the guard's relationship to the attack, the distance, a late decision, or poor execution? Do not diagnose every failure as “wrong guard.”

    Easier: One known attacking line, slower entries, larger starting distance, and only two guard choices. Harder: Both target lines remain live; B may first threaten a blade engagement, but still gets only one committed attack. Raise uncertainty before increasing pace.

    Debrief and one useful intervention

    0:55–1:03. Ask each pair: “Which option really existed? What did the opponent do that you did not expect? Did you have to reload before answering?”

    Choose one recurring problem, demonstrate one change, then retest for several exchanges. Examples: adjust the point so it relates to the opponent; shorten an unnecessary drop before cutting; move the feet so a low invitation is not simply a reachable head; or leave instead of trying to force an unavailable riposte.

    Ask for the student’s observation first, then give one cue linked to the guard problem and retest it.

    Change without a reset

    1:03–1:10. In solo lanes, trace Tag → Longpoint → Alber slowly through a descending cut, then Zornhut → Longpoint → Wechsel through a diagonal cut. These are movement relationships Meyer describes, not mandatory finishing routes in a bout. [S1, fols. 9v–10r]

    Repeat the cut but stop where the current task requires; do not drive the point into the floor or carry every live cut to the lowest diagram position. Then let a partner show a known high cut. Use a familiar hanging cover and either answer if the line is truly open or leave under cover. No compulsory zwerch or blind riposte.

    Question: “Which guard did the action carry you through, and could you act again from the result?” This is the bridge from named positions to continuous fencing. It is not a request to dance through guards in striking range.

    Game 2: Offer, read, leave

    1:10–1:30. The purpose is to use a guard as information: suggest an opportunity, see what the opponent actually does, and act without betting everything on a predicted response.

    Setup: Keep the short exchanges and one immediate answer from Game 1. Choose cuts-only or the club’s usual thrust-inclusive rules before the set. Start outside immediate reach. Both may use familiar attacks, defenses, and footwork. A chooses a guard or one deliberate guard transition and privately tells the observer what response they hope to invite. B is free to attack, defend, change measure, engage the blade, or wait.

    A's task: Make a credible offer while remaining ready. Take the opening if it is real; defend if the opponent attacks differently; reset or leave if nothing useful develops. There is no requirement to change guards after the offer. B's task is to fence successfully, not make A's idea work.

    Two starting examples: From a low guard outside reach, invite a high attack and prepare a familiar cover-and-answer or cover-and-exit. From Longpoint at safe measure, invite a blade search and be ready to avoid the search or defend its follow-up. Neither is guaranteed. If they close behind a real threat instead, solve that threat first.

    How to run it: Allow three minutes to demonstrate, agree targets, and choose the first intention. Run three four-minute sets with a one-minute debrief after each; switch A/B after two minutes. Use the final two minutes to remove the assigned initiator and let either fencer offer the problem. Rotate partners between sets if space permits.

    Scoring and observation: Use the same clean-hit scoring as Game 1. Do not award points for visibly changing guard or making the prediction come true. The observer records whether B gave the expected response and whether A selected an appropriate answer to the response that actually happened. A sound decision that produces no hit can still be good learning; a double cannot be a successful trap.

    Easier: Limit B to two genuinely different responses, such as attacking the offered high line or declining. Harder: Remove the declared offer, allow either person to initiate, and vary the starting distance. Discuss how removing thrusts changes the invitation from Ochs, Pflug, or Longpoint: the point’s scoring threat is absent in a cuts-only set.

    Open fencing and transfer

    1:30–1:55. Use five four-minute rounds with one minute of water, partner changes, and brief observation after each round, including the final round before reflection. Use the action rules selected for Game 2, with no bonus scoring or mandatory guards.

    Ask students to carry one intention into a round: “I will use my starting position to make the first action simpler,” or “I will stop changing position once the opening is already there.” An observer can name the opening relationship after the exchange, not shout guard names during it.

    Look for less unnecessary preparation, fewer freezes after the first action, selective use of invitations, and safer finishes. More guard changes or more touches alone do not demonstrate improvement. Keep score if useful, but compare the quality of decisions, not just totals.

    Guard reflection

    1:55–2:00. Ask each student for one guard they used, one response it actually produced, and one occasion when they changed or abandoned the plan. Then ask: “What would you change if the same opponent simply waited?”

    For the next delivery, note whether students could identify the six core positions, which invitations produced the expected response, and whether clean finishes improved from Game 1 to open fencing. Keep naming errors separate from functional failures; both matter, but they need different coaching.
