    Decisive Action I

    How can I see a real opening, choose a safe plausible answer, and commit before I lose the moment searching for certainty?

    LESSON | INSTRUCTORS AUDIENCE

    Chris Mineweaser | July 2026 | Version 1.2

    ← All classes and lessons

    Class context: 120 MINUTES | MODERN PERFORMANCE LESSON

    The problem, behavior, and boundaries

    A decision lesson that helps students make timely, trained choices against a live opponent. It does not ask them to react randomly or to chase an imaginary perfect answer.

    Doing Something Is Better Than Nothing

    CENTRAL QUESTION: How can I recognize a meaningful cue, choose from a small prepared menu, and act before the window closes?

    END-OF-CLASS BEHAVIOR: Students identify a real invitation to act, commit to one safe prepared answer without waiting for complete certainty, then recover or exit rather than freezing or flailing.

    PRIMARY COACHING IDEA: Fast decisions are not random decisions. Strong fencers pick up useful information sooner, narrow the menu, and act from a practiced answer.

    SOURCE NOTE: Deliberately modern. Pair it with the current technical curriculum rather than attaching a historical source where one does not belong.

    SAFETY BOUNDARY: A decision must remain controlled, structurally sound, and appropriate to the partner. Panic swings, reckless entries, and continuing after a clear failure are not successes.

    What the fencing evidence actually supports

    The useful evidence is about choice load, sport-specific information, and transfer. It does not show that a faster first motion or more technical options will solve the problem.

    Study 1 - choice load in quasi-realistic fencing

    Milic and colleagues compared 10 beginners with 10 experienced national-team fencers. Participants saw fencing-specific video stimuli and made simple, two-choice, or four-choice offensive/defensive responses. Reaction time rose as the number of stimulus-response alternatives rose. Across conditions, beginners were 25.5% to 34.8% slower; their reaction-time increase as options grew was also steeper (35.2% to 55.2%).

    WHAT THE STUDY TESTED WHAT IT FOUND WHAT THIS CLASS BORROWS
    One known response; then 2 and 4 possible response alternatives. 4-choice > 2-choice > simple; beginners were slower in every condition. Start with two practiced answers. Vary timing and distance before adding techniques.

    Study result summarized above: Milic et al. (2020), Figure 3 and Figure 4.

    Study 2 - transfer, not trick performance

    A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies (45 effect sizes) found perceptual-cognitive training improved controlled laboratory tasks more strongly than real-game performance: ES = 1.51 in the laboratory and ES = 0.65 in real play. The transfer effect was still positive, but materially smaller.

    THE TAKEAWAY: Clean drills are the start of the lesson, not proof that the skill transferred. Restore real information quickly: a moving opponent, genuine measure, variable timing, and a consequence for waiting.

    The conversation to have before fencing

    Keep the claim human and precise. The point is not to make students think less; it is to give thinking a fast, usable structure.

    1. Normalize the problem

    In a zero-sum bout, both people are trying to act before the other does. Freezing while searching for the perfect answer is a normal combat-sport problem, not evidence that someone lacks courage or talent.

    2. Define the goal

    We are not trying to make you swing faster. We are trying to help you recognize a real cue, choose a practiced answer, and commit while the action is still available.

    3. Give the rule

    When you see a real cue, choose one safe, plausible response from the menu. A technically acceptable answer on time is worth more than a perfect plan that arrives after the exchange is over.

    4. Protect safety

    This does not authorize panic. If the cue is not real, keep measure. If the answer fails, recover or leave. The standard is controlled commitment, not blind aggression.

    5. Explain today’s score

    A point can count even if the hit does not land when the student saw the cue and made a clean, appropriate decision before freezing. Open fencing later tests whether that behavior survives.

    REPEAT ALL CLASS: See something real. Pick one answer. Commit cleanly. Recover if it is wrong.

    Exact 120-minute plan

    The lesson progresses from known answers to real choice, then releases the behavior into score pressure and open fencing.

    TIME BLOCK PLAN FOR THIS CLASS OUTPUT
    0:00-0:10 Talk + orientation Name the decision problem; set the two-answer menu and safety boundary. Shared language
    0:10-0:20 Decision-ready movement Enter, brake, and leave measure on a real visual cue. Usable body positions
    0:20-0:35 Known-answer rehearsal Build two familiar, safe answers before forcing selection. Both answers available
    0:35-0:50 Cue-and-commit Read one of two distinct entries and act before the feeder completes it. Cue linked to action
    0:50-1:10 Constraint game I Give delay a cost while protecting cue quality and recovery. Timely, controlled choices
    1:10-1:30 Constraint game II Vary measure, preparation, and recovery without enlarging the technical menu. Transfer under uncertainty
    1:30-1:45 Scenario bouts Apply score pressure without rewarding panic or random attacks. Composure under pressure
    1:45-1:55 Open fencing Notice whether the class behavior appears without bonus scoring. Real transfer
    1:55-2:00 Debrief Name a useful cue and the next small decision menu. Next action

    TIME CHECK: 10 + 10 + 15 + 15 + 20 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 5 = 120 minutes.

    TRANSITION PLAN: Students gear before the opening. Pair by compatible control and size. Keep masks and gloves on from cue-and-commit through open fencing. Use one audible timer and announce rotations before each interval ends.

    Move from information, not anticipation

    E | BLOCK 1 | 0:10-0:20

    Before anyone chooses a fencing action, their feet must be free to move. This block does not teach the two sword answers yet. It teaches the simpler habit underneath them: wait for something real, then move with balance.

    THE POINT IN PLAIN ENGLISH: Do not decide while nothing is happening. Stay balanced until your partner actually changes the distance. If they take space toward you, leave or make room. If they give space away, take space under control. The exact sword actions come in the next block.

    1. PARTNER MIRROR: ENTER, BRAKE, LEAVE (4 MIN)

    One fencer leads with small advances, retreats, and angle changes. The follower mirrors while staying in a fenced posture. On the leader's hand signal, both stop and hold their balance for one beat. Switch at 2:00. This is the warm-up for seeing movement without guessing.

    2. TAKE SPACE OR LEAVE SPACE (6 MIN)

    Stay at sword-safe distance with no cuts. The leader chooses one honest movement: step forward to close distance, or step back to give distance. The follower waits for the step, then makes one matching answer: angle out and reset when space closes, or take one small forward step when space opens. Reset after every repetition; switch at 3:00.

    A successful repetition looks like this

    • The follower waits for an actual change in distance rather than moving on a guess.
    • The first step fits what the partner did: make room when they close, take space when they give it.
    • Both fencers finish balanced enough to stop, change direction, or add a sword action next.

    PARTNER RULE: The leader may wait as long as they like before making a real step. Do not fake cues. The follower resets if they move before the step or remain frozen after it.

    Build two answers before demanding a choice

    F | BLOCK 2 | 0:20-0:35

    This is the whole decision menu for Decisive Action I - not a list of every good longsword answer. Students rehearse two familiar answers so the later decision is between two usable actions, not every possibility they know.

    ANSWER A - COVER AND EXIT: Use this when the opponent is already entering with a committed, threatening line. The immediate job is to protect yourself, remove the line, and leave at an angle. Success is not a pretty counter; it is ending the exchange safe and balanced.

    ANSWER B - TAKE THE OPENING: Use this when the opponent has given a real opening before their action is fully established. Enter with one simple, controlled action, then recover or exit. Success is acting on the opening without staying in front of their return.

    WHY ONLY TWO? A small menu makes the lesson about recognition and commitment. Later classes can add more actions. Today, adding options would only make it harder to see whether the student recognized the cue or simply got lost choosing.

    KNOWN-CUE REHEARSAL: In pairs, the feeder gives the same committed entry each time. The actor performs A five times, then B five times. The feeder keeps the picture honest and safe; the actor finishes every answer with posture and an exit.

    HOLD HERE: This block confirms that both answers are actually usable before asking a student to recognize when either one belongs.

    Cue-and-commit

    G | BLOCK 3 | 0:35-0:50

    The opponent owns the cue. The actor does not know which answer is appropriate until meaningful information appears.

    SETUP: Begin at a range where either partner can make a composed, one-exchange action. The feeder may use two honest entries: one clearly calling for Answer A and one clearly inviting Answer B. Make them visually distinct at first.

    REPETITION: The feeder moves naturally, chooses an entry, and commits enough to make the cue readable. The actor recognizes, selects A or B, completes the action, and exits. Reset each exchange. Switch after 8-10 repetitions.

    SUCCESS STANDARD: The actor gets the repetition for a safe, appropriate choice before the feeder completes the entry - even if the final hit is imperfect. No credit for pre-empting a non-cue, changing answers halfway through, or freezing until contact.

    Coach prompts

    • Which cue did you see?
    • Which answer fit it?
    • Were you still safe at that measure?
    • Did you leave after the decision?

    NEXT STEP: Once both choices are clean, give the feeder a neutral preparation before either entry. The actor must now wait for information without becoming passive.

    The Window Closes

    GAME CARD 1

    Give delay a real cost without asking anyone to charge forward. The game rewards timely commitment and recovery, not merely who lands first.

    STARTING STATE: Fencers begin in measure. One is the initiator and one is the responder. The initiator may use genuine preparation, then chooses either class picture. The responder’s job is to recognize and use A or B. After 30 seconds, switch roles and rotate partners often.

    PLAYER GOALS: The responder identifies an honest cue and makes a safe class answer before the initiator finishes a committed action.

    RULES AND CONSTRAINTS: Responder scores 2 for a timely, safe answer with clean exit or recovery. Initiator scores 1 if the responder freezes through a clear cue or chooses an unsafe/nonsensical action. No point for fake/noncommittal pictures, pre-emption before an honest cue, or loss of control.

    SCORING: Score the class behavior separately from the hit. The goal is an informed answer on time, not random first motion.

    DOSE AND ROTATION: Two 4-minute role blocks, one partner change, and 2 minutes to reset rules and debrief.

    COACH OBSERVES: Waiting through an honest cue, early guessing, switching answers halfway through, forgotten exits, and any action that becomes unsafe under speed.

    PROGRESSION: Let the initiator change preparation tempo or delay the moment of commitment.

    REGRESSION: Return to unmistakable entries and slightly slower speed; retain the same two answers.

    Add uncertainty, not chaos

    H | BLOCK 5 | 1:10-1:30

    Increase one variable at a time. If quality collapses, return to the last version that still looked like fencing.

    Level 1 - Clear entries

    The feeder’s two choices are deliberately distinct. The actor learns what each cue feels like.

    Level 2 - Variable preparation

    The feeder may change tempo, take a small step, or wait longer before either entry. The actor may not launch early.

    Level 3 - Variable measure

    Start one step farther or closer. The same answer must now be scaled to the distance actually present.

    Level 4 - Honest counterpressure

    After the actor’s response, the feeder may recover or defend safely. The actor exits or recovers rather than expecting the exchange to stop.

    COACH DECISION: Do not add feints, lower-line work, or three new technical answers just because the drill becomes easy. This is Decisive Action I. Complexity comes from timing, distance, and an opponent - not an unlimited decision tree.

    Scenario bouts

    I | BLOCK 6 | 1:30-1:45

    Scenario pressure restores the reason people hesitate: score, consequence, and the feeling that one decision matters. Keep it short enough that the class behavior remains visible.

    SCENARIO CONSTRAINT WHAT YOU ARE WATCHING
    Down 3-1 Responder has 45 seconds to create or take one honest decision window. They may not turtle indefinitely. Do they act from information or force an action from anxiety?
    Protecting a lead Initiator is up 3-1 but cannot retreat out of all range. They must remain able to give or take a real cue. Does score pressure make the fencer passive, early, or still composed?
    One clean exchange Each fencer receives one meaningful exchange. A clean, timely choice counts even without a perfect hit. Can they preserve the class behavior when the moment feels scarce?
    COACH RESPONSE Do not punish missed decisions with extra conditioning or public criticism. Name the information, name the available answer, and run the situation again. This is decision training, not shame training.

    Open fencing and debrief

    J | BLOCK 7 | 1:45-1:55

    The final portion is the test, not the whole lesson. Students may use any safe, appropriate fencing; watch whether the class behavior survives without game rules.

    OPEN FENCING: Run normal short bouts. Before each bout, each fencer names one personal cue they intend to recognize: for example, committed high entry, opponent freezing after a feint, or opponent giving ground with extended hands. They do not need to force it; they are responsible for noticing it.

    Debrief questions

    1. Where did you see a real cue and act before you had full certainty?

    2. Where did you wait for the perfect answer and lose the moment?

    3. What should the next lesson do: make the cue clearer, make the answer cleaner, or add one new

    branch?

    CLOSE: Decisive does not mean reckless. It means your preparation lets you make a controlled answer while there is still time.

    Keep the lesson honest

    K | COACHING LANGUAGE

    This lesson can accidentally reward two bad outcomes: acting before information, or rewarding any action simply because it was fast. Keep feedback tied to cue, choice, structure, and recovery.

    WHEN YOU SEE SAY OR ASK NEXT COACHING MOVE
    Freeze after an honest cue What did you see? Which of the two answers was available? Return to the clear-entry version; make the cue unmistakable before restoring speed.
    Early action before a cue That was a guess. What information would make that answer appropriate? Let the feeder delay longer. Reward waiting for the cue, not guessing faster.
    Half-committed action You chose, then changed your mind halfway. Was the menu too big or was the answer not ready? Reduce to one cue/two answers; rehearse physical exit again.
    Reckless action Speed is not the goal. Can you make that same decision with structure and partner control? Slow down and rebuild quality. Do not award the repetition.
    Clean on-time miss Good decision. What adjustment would make the finish better? Award the decision separately from the technical finish; run it again with a tighter constraint.

    What this lesson is not

    L | FAILURE MODES

    The distinction matters. Decisive action is a trainable information-action link, not an excuse for panic or a demand to move before the opponent gives anything real.

    Doing something becomes random aggression

    Correct the phrase: a safe, plausible, trained response is better than freezing. Require an honest cue and a controlled recovery.

    Students wait for a verbal prompt

    Use the opponent’s body, weapon, timing, and measure as information. The coach may freeze or replay, but should not become the source of every decision.

    The feeder is too cooperative

    If every cue is huge and every response lands, the actor is memorizing. Give the feeder real preparation and an honest recovery without turning it into uncontrolled free fencing too soon.

    Too many technical options

    Return to two answers. A larger decision tree is an advanced progression, not proof of sophistication.

    Score pressure creates panic

    Reduce consequence while preserving the cue. Ask for one clean exchange rather than five points, then reintroduce score pressure gradually.

    VERSION 1.2 CHOICE: Keep the first lesson narrow. We are building a habit of committed, informed action - not a library of clever answers.
