    Efficient Cutting I

    How can I make a real cut arrive quickly, stay covered, and leave me ready for the next action?

    LESSON | INSTRUCTORS AUDIENCE

    Chris Mineweaser | August 2026 | Version 1.0

    ← All classes and lessons

    Class context: 120 MINUTES | MEYER + LIECHTENAUER LENS

    The problem, behavior, and boundaries

    This class develops compact diagonal cuts to the upper openings: launch from a usable forward position, cut with real edge alignment and body support, then arrest, redirect, cover, or leave without a large reset. It does not reward wrist taps, uncontrolled doubles, or huge cutting combinations.

    CENTRAL QUESTION: How can I make a real cut arrive quickly, stay covered, and leave me ready for the next action?

    END-OF-CLASS BEHAVIOR: Students make a credible diagonal cut by using only as much path as the target requires. They finish balanced, with the sword still between them and the opponent, and choose a second cut only when the first action actually leaves one available.

    HISTORICAL ANCHOR: Meyer's quick short-edge continuations and the Liechtenauer glosses' immediate actions after blade contact. The sources support prompt, protected continuation; the lesson's compact-cut mechanics, exercise choices, and tournament success test are modern coaching adaptations.

    Target-action success test

    The target behavior counts only when all four observable conditions are present.

    REQUIREMENT OBSERVABLE STANDARD
    Threat If the opponent leaves the upper opening open, the cut can land cleanly with recognizable edge alignment and safe control.
    Economy The sword starts from a usable forward relationship and reaches the target without a large backward windup, shoulder-driven loop, or excessive travel beyond the opening.
    Readiness The fencer can arrest or redirect the sword while remaining balanced and covered, rather than needing a separate reset before the next decision.
    Judgment The fencer links a second cut only when it is still available; otherwise they recover, cover, or leave without accepting the return.

    NO DOUBLE: A result that leaves both fencers validly hit does not satisfy the lesson, even if one blade arrived first.

    Primary functional model

    Begin with the sword present enough to threaten. Use the hands and forearms to organize the line, with the torso and feet supporting the action rather than adding a large shoulder windup. The grip firms only enough to control the arrival. Finish with posture, point, and hilt still useful; then either change to the next real opening or recover under cover.

    Instructor hypothesis

    Students will often load behind the body, lead with a tense shoulder, flick loosely at the target, cut so far past it that the sword must travel back before it can defend, or throw a second cut by habit when the opponent has already recovered. Some will mistake frantic speed for sharpness and score an initial touch while accepting the immediate return.

    Arrival and orientation

    C | BLOCK 1 | 0:00-0:05

    Students understand the problem, success test, and class arc.

    OPENING SENTENCE: "Tonight we are solving one problem: How can I make a real cut arrive quickly, stay covered, and leave me ready for the next action?"

    SUCCESS DEFINITION: State the four-part success test in plain language and make clear that a double is a failed solution.

    CLASS ARC: Experience the problem first; compare what worked; examine one historical lens; increase choice and pressure; test transfer in open fencing.

    DO NOT: Teach the complete historical solution before the first game. Students should possess the problem before receiving the model.

    Athletic development

    C | BLOCK 2 | 0:05-0:20

    Build the ability to accelerate, decelerate, and redirect the sword through a stable shoulder, rotating forearm, controlled grip, and balanced base - without stiffness or fatigue.

    WHAT THIS BLOCK IS: A short movement lesson, not conditioning. Demonstrate each exercise once, keep the volume low, and stop or regress when posture or landing quality deteriorates.

    TARGET QUALITY: Build the ability to accelerate, decelerate, and redirect the sword through a stable shoulder, rotating forearm, controlled grip, and balanced base - without stiffness or fatigue.

    Exercise sequence

    EXERCISE DOSE COACHING FOCUS MOVEMENT PURPOSE

    Forearm rotation map 2 x 6 each side Elbows quiet; wrist neutral Organizes edge and hand position

    How to perform the exercises

    C | BLOCK 2 | CONTINUED

    Keep the movements low-volume and precise. The goal is controllable redirection, not fatigue.

    1. SCAPULAR SET AND REACH

    Stand in fencing stance with empty hands. Let the shoulder blades settle gently down and around the ribs, then reach both hands forward as though presenting the hilt and point. Return without pinching the shoulder blades together or lifting the shoulders to the ears. Cue: 'Long neck; live hands.'

    2. FOREARM ROTATION MAP

    Hold the elbows comfortably in front of the ribs at about a right angle. Slowly rotate the forearms so the palms travel inward, downward, then outward again, keeping the wrists straight and grip relaxed. The goal is control, not range. Cue: 'Turn the forearm, not the whole shoulder.'

    3. SHADOW CUT AND FREEZE

    Without a sword, trace a diagonal cut from a forward hand position toward an imaginary upper opening. Freeze at arrival for one second with the chest upright and hands still usable, then return by the shortest safe path. Alternate sides. Cue: 'Arrive; do not fall through.'

    4. STEP, CUT, AND STICK

    With a trainer and full room awareness, begin just outside a partner's measure. On an agreed opening cue, take one controlled approach step and deliver a light diagonal cut to a legal protected target or a safe target substitute. Freeze for one second in the finish before moving again. Do not chase speed; own the endpoint. Cue: 'One step, one cut, one ready shape.'

    REGRESSION AND STOP RULE: Reduce speed, distance, or impact; replace jumps with controlled steps. Stop for pain, dizziness, repeated loss of balance, loud uncontrolled landings, or a student who cannot maintain the described posture.

    Representative problem exposure: Cut and Recover

    C | BLOCK 3 | 0:20-0:40

    Establish a real but controlled first cut, and reveal whether it leaves the attacker capable of protecting themselves immediately afterward.

    STARTING STATE: Partners work in a lane at the edge of one-step cutting distance. The defender begins in a mobile high guard and, on the attacker’s approach, either leaves one upper opening genuinely available for a beat or closes it with a simple high cover. The attacker may make one diagonal cut only.

    PLAYER GOALS: Attacker chooses a compact diagonal cut only when the upper line is truly open, lands with controlled structure, then returns to a usable forward cover. Defender gives an honest opening or cover and may make one light return only against an attacker who overtravels or hangs unprotected.

    RULES AND CONSTRAINTS: The attacker gets one approach and one cut. If the defender closes the line in time, the correct answer is to recover or leave - not to strike through the cover. Keep impacts light and stop after the first clean outcome or safe separation.

    SCORING: Clean cut to an open line plus usable recovery 2; correct abort or covered exit when the line closes 1; defender return against an overextended finish 1; loose tap, forced cut into cover, double, or accepted return 0-0.

    DOSE AND ROTATION: 4 minutes each role; change partner and repeat on the opposite diagonal. Use the final 2 minutes of the block to compare what made one cut feel ready and another feel stranded.

    INSTRUCTOR OBSERVES: Large backward windups, shoulder tension, edge drift, feet arriving after the sword, sword traveling far past the target, and attackers who only learn about their recovery when the defender hits them back.

    COACH POSTURE: Observe across the room before intervening. Record common patterns and let several exchanges occur before deciding what the class needs.

    Guided coaching

    C | BLOCK 4 | 0:40-0:55

    Use the first game's evidence to provide the smallest intervention that improves the class.

    Debrief questions

    1. Where did your sword start before the cut, and how much of that path was actually necessary?

    2. What made the cut real rather than a loose wrist tap?

    3. Could you protect yourself or change lines from the position where the cut ended?

    4. When the opening closed, what told you to stop rather than force the cut?

    5. Which part of the action made you feel slow: the launch, the arrival, or the recovery?

    PRIMARY INTERVENTION: Demonstrate the same diagonal cut three ways: a large shoulder windup, a loose flick, and a compact cut that reaches the target and finishes ready. Keep the language simple: 'Start present. Cut through the opening. Finish useful.' Then show that a second cut is a choice made from the first finish, not a mandatory pattern.

    VARIABLE REHEARSAL: Partners re-test the target problem at approximately 60-70% intensity. Keep at least one meaningful variable - side, rhythm, distance, pressure, or response - rather than feeding identical repetitions.

    FEEDBACK PLAN: Use one cue at a time. Correct immediate danger at once; otherwise give feedback between sets of three attempts. Ask the student to identify the information that shaped the action.

    AVOID: Turning the block into a long lecture or adding several fixes before students can test the first one.

    Historical integration: Meyer + Liechtenauer Lens

    C | BLOCK 5 | 0:55-1:05

    Connect the experienced tactical problem to a documented historical principle without claiming that the source prescribes the modern game.

    SOURCE IDENTIFICATION: Meyer's quick short-edge continuations and the Liechtenauer glosses' immediate actions after blade contact. The sources support prompt, protected continuation; the lesson's compact-cut mechanics, exercise choices, and tournament success test are modern coaching adaptations.

    VERIFIED HISTORICAL CLAIM: Meyer includes several short-edge continuations to be performed quickly after a prior action, including the After-cut from Below; the Liechtenauer glosses likewise describe striking again immediately from the sword after a clash. Together, these sources support prompt action that remains connected to a threatening line. They do not prescribe the exact modern cut size, speed test, or physical training used here. [1] [2]

    MODERN TRAINING INTERPRETATION: Use a slow crosswise-cut or short-edge continuation as the historical example: the hands and cross stay protective while the weapon changes line quickly. Then show the modern lesson translation: a diagonal cut should not need a giant loop before it can threaten again. The goal is not to make every cut small; it is to choose the smallest path that can still produce a real, defensible threat.

    DEMONSTRATION: Show three slow examples: one successful application, one common failure, and one case in which the action should be declined. Let students rehearse briefly, then return to live choice.

    EVIDENCE LINE: Historical text supplies the cited principle or action. The success test, games, scoring, cues, and safety adaptations are modern coaching design.

    Build the mechanics behind the cut

    A short 2-3x/week routine for shoulder stability, forearm control, grip balance, trunk control, and controlled solo cutting. This is practice, not a speed test.

    Keep every repetition smooth. Begin with no load or a very light object; use a waster or synthetic trainer for solo cutting only in a clear space, never near people, pets, glass, or low ceilings. Stop if wrist, elbow, or shoulder pain increases.

    1. SCAPULAR WALL SLIDES - 2 X 8

    Stand with your back near a wall, ribs down, and slide the arms upward only as far as the shoulders stay comfortable. Do not force the elbows or shrug. This builds a stable overhead/forward shoulder position.

    2. LIGHT FOREARM ROTATIONS - 2 X 8 EACH

    Hold a light hammer, dowel, or similar object with the elbow by your side. Slowly rotate palm-up to palm-down without bending the wrist. Use so little weight that you can stop anywhere in the path.

    3. FINGER-EXTENSOR OPENS - 2 X 12

    Put a light rubber band around the fingers and thumb, then open the hand smoothly against it. This balances frequent gripping work and encourages the hands to stay less clenched between cuts.

    4. SPLIT-STANCE ANTI-ROTATION PRESS - 2 X 8 EACH

    With a light resistance band at chest height, stand in fencing stance and press straight forward without letting the torso twist. If no band is available, use an isometric palms-together press for 10 seconds. The trunk should support the cut, not spin ahead of it.

    5. SOLO CUT-TO-FREEZE - 3 X 4 EACH DIAGONAL

    Use empty hands first, then a safe trainer if the space permits. Deliver one diagonal cut at roughly 50- 60% speed and freeze in the end position for one second. Check: balanced feet, no shoulder shrug, sword still able to cover or redirect.

    6. TWO-CUT FLOW - 2 X 4 EACH DIRECTION

    Link one diagonal cut to the opposite upper opening only if the first finish makes it available. Keep the path small and quiet; stop immediately if the sequence becomes a big loop or the grip locks up. Once a week, film two repetitions from the side and look for the reset you are trying to remove.

    SIMPLE WEEKLY PLAN: Run items 1-4 as a 10-minute body-prep circuit twice per week. Add items 5-6 for 5-8 minutes on one or two separate days. Speed belongs at the end of a well-controlled progression, never before it.

    Progressive constraints

    C | BLOCK 6 | 1:05-1:30

    Increase uncertainty while preserving the lesson's central information-action relationship.

    Game 2 - One Cut, Two Windows | 12 minutes

    Make the attacker select a compact cut for the opening that actually appears, while the defender tests whether the cut can be recovered safely.

    • At edge measure, the defender invites one upper line, closes it, or gives ground. The initiator uses footwork, then makes one diagonal cut followed by recovery or cover.

    • Initiator takes only a real upper window with no extra windup and survives the response. Defender returns against overtravel or poor recovery.

    • One scoring cut only. Defender may use distance or a high cover, but not a pre-frozen parry. Count only a stable forward cover.

    • Selected cut plus covered recovery 2; covered no-cut or exit 1; defender return 1; wrong window, loose tap, double, or accepted return 0.

    • 5 minutes each role; 2 minutes for explanation and reset.

    Game 3 - Cut, Change, or Leave | 13 minutes

    A sharp first cut becomes a second only when the defender genuinely leaves the second opening available.

    • Free onset just outside measure. Initiator may cut to either upper opening; defender parries, retreats, or covers and returns once. Initiator may redirect once or leave.

    • Initiator makes a real first cut, then reads the result. Change lines only when the opening exists; defender punishes overtravel and scripting.

    • Maximum two cuts. The second needs a real path; no looped reset. Halt on clean score, double, or safe separation.

    • First cut plus safe finish 2; compact redirection plus finish 3; covered leave 2; defender hit 1; forced second, double, or afterblow 0.

    • Change initiator every 3 minutes; release the role in the final minute.

    PROGRESSION TRIGGER: Either fencer may create the compact-cut sequence in normal onset.

    REGRESSION TRIGGER: Return to one known side and cover. Add the second cut only after the opening is clear.

    Open sparring

    C | BLOCK 7 | 1:30-1:55

    Test whether the behavior appears without forced actions or bonus scoring.

    FORMAT: Five 4-minute rounds with approximately 1 minute between rounds for rotation and brief feedback. Use normal open-fencing rules and the club's usual intensity agreement.

    SHARED OBJECTIVE: "Fence normally. Seek clean upper-opening and hand opportunities, but make each cut only as large as the window needs. A sharp cut is one that can score, keep you covered, and leave you ready to solve the next exchange."

    PAIRING PLAN: Use compatible control and size, then rotate across different rhythms and preferences. Supervise novice pairings and avoid a large mismatch solely to complete the rotation.

    Coach records

    • Sword begins usable and forward enough to threaten
    • Diagonal path and edge alignment are clear
    • Shoulder stays mobile; torso and feet support the action
    • Can arrest, cover, or redirect from the finish
    • Second cut appears only when the defender leaves it available
    • Clean score survives the immediate return
    • Uses compactness selectively at appropriate measure

    FEEDBACK: Observe during rounds and coach mainly between them. Open sparring is not another constrained game.

    Reflection and close

    C | BLOCK 8 | 1:55-2:00

    Students consolidate one usable cue and one testable next action.

    Whole-group questions

    1. What made a cut feel sharp without making it reckless?

    2. Which part of your body created extra motion that did not help the target?

    3. When did the first cut genuinely leave a second cut available - and when did leaving become the better

    answer?

    1. What did the defender do that exposed an overlong or late recovery?

    INDIVIDUAL NEXT ACTION: Each student states one small, observable action they will test the next time this problem appears.

    INSTRUCTOR CLOSE: Restate the functional success test, not a technique name. Record any safety issue, equipment problem, or student who needs a lower-intensity follow-up.

    END ON TIME: Do not let the final sparring round consume reflection. The class is incomplete until students name the information that shaped their decision.

    Plan modifications before the room forces them

    Preserve the information and decision while reducing uncertainty, speed, distance, or impact.

    CONDITION PLANNED RESPONSE
    First-time student Use a known attack or response, longer preparation time, controlled partner, and score safe resolution as success.
    Mixed experience Give beginners less uncertainty; give experienced students more choice or rhythm variation, not greater impact.
    Odd attendance Use attacker, defender, and observer. Observer applies the four-part success test, then roles rotate.
    Limited space Use narrow lanes; prohibit passing and large leaps; emphasize compact footwork and controlled stops.
    Injury / low impact Replace bounds with steps; reduce speed and contact; permit observer or limited-role participation.
    Large attendance Create lanes, assign a senior student to each cluster, and synchronize changes with one timer.
    Game breaks down Remove one variable, increase distance, slow the action, or return to a known starting state.
    Time loss Compress explanation and one role interval; preserve open transfer and reflection.

    What to watch without interrupting every exchange

    Use the checklist to distinguish learning from mere scoring.

    DOMAIN LOOK FOR COMMON FAILURE
    Start position Sword begins usable and forward enough to threaten Loads behind body or requires a visible windup
    Line and edge Diagonal path and edge alignment are clear Wrist tap, vague line, or floppy edge
    Structure Shoulder stays mobile; torso and feet support the action Tense shrug, shoulder-led loop, or collapsing posture
    Endpoint Can arrest, cover, or redirect from the finish Sword overtravels and needs a reset
    Choice Second cut appears only when the defender leaves it available Runs a memorized double-cut regardless of the parry
    Outcome Clean score survives the immediate return Counts the first touch despite afterblow or double
    Transfer Uses compactness selectively at appropriate measure Makes all cuts tiny or chases hand taps without threat

    Between-round feedback formula

    Observation -> question -> one cue -> re-test. Name what happened, ask what information the student perceived, offer one change, and let the next round test it.

    Class-level success evidence

    • Cleaner solutions and fewer doubles from Game 1 to Game 3.
    • Students can name at least one reliable cue.
    • The target behavior appears selectively in open fencing.
    • Students can explain why an unsafe score does not satisfy the lesson.
    • Beginners show safer structure even when they do not score.
